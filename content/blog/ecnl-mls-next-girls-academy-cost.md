If you've spent any time researching competitive youth soccer in the United States, three names show up over and over: **ECNL**, **MLS Next**, and **Girls Academy**. They look like a list of three competitive tiers. They're actually **two co-equal top tiers** with deliberately overlapping footprints — and the cost picture only makes sense once you see the structure correctly.

This piece is a walk through what each one actually is, what each costs at the family level, and what the cumulative bill looks like by the time a player has run the full pipeline.

It is not a buyer's guide. It is a piece for families and donors who want to understand where the dollars go, and for anyone trying to figure out whether a kid they know is being asked to absorb a reasonable household commitment.

(For a deeper walk through the tier structure itself, see [ECNL and MLS Next / Girls Academy: how the competitive tier structure actually works](/blog/competitive-tier-structure-explained).)

## The two top tiers

**Tier 1 — MLS Next + Girls Academy** (gender-paired)

- **MLS Next** is the **boys'** competitive pathway operated by Major League Soccer. Roughly 150 boys' clubs participate. Standards are tightly enforced — minimum training hours, minimum coaching credentials, performance-based promotion and relegation between divisions. National events bring U.S. Soccer Federation evaluators and college scouts. Roster size is typically 16–20. Within MLS Next, **MLS Next Academy** programs are operated by MLS-affiliated professional club academies, and the **Homegrown** designation identifies players signed from those academies into MLS first-team or reserve contracts.
- **Girls Academy (GA)** is the **girls'** competitive pathway, formed in 2020 by U.S. Soccer-sanctioned clubs after the U.S. Soccer Development Academy (USSDA) shut down. Roughly 70–80 girls' clubs participate. Showcase structure and recruiting visibility mirror MLS Next on the girls' side. GA's secondary tier, **GA Aspire**, operates as a developmental layer beneath the top GA flight.

**Tier 2 — ECNL** (single league, both genders)

- **ECNL (Elite Clubs National League)** operates **both boys' and girls'** competitive programs under one league framework. Roughly 150+ clubs on the girls' side, fewer on the boys' side. Clubs are independent member organizations — ECNL is owned and run by its member clubs, not by a senior professional league. Showcase events bring college scouts. Roster size per team is typically 18–22. **ECRL (ECNL Regional League)** runs as ECNL's secondary tier on both the boys' and girls' sides — same competitive framework, lower travel intensity and household cost than top ECNL.

Both tiers operate at the **U13–U18/U19** competitive age bands. Both are considered top-tier nationally. Below them, several secondary and developmental leagues run strong competitive play at lower household cost: **ECRL** as the secondary ECNL tier, **GA Aspire** as the secondary GA tier, **NPL** (National Premier Leagues, both sides), **DPL** (Discovery Player League, girls' developmental), **Elite Academy** (boys' developmental), and the **SoCal Flight System** (California's regional Flight 1 / 2 / 3 ranking structure). Most San Diego competitive clubs operate teams at several of these levels concurrently.

## What the tuition looks like

Tuition at top-tier clubs participating in either ecosystem in San Diego County runs roughly **$3,500 to $5,500 per season** for the competitive program, though some clubs charge meaningfully more. Multi-season annual commitments — fall, winter futsal, spring, summer — push the per-year tuition into the **$4,500 to $8,500 range** for a full year of competitive play. Cost differences between specific clubs within each tier are usually larger than cost differences between the tiers themselves.

This is the _registration line_. It does not include kit. It does not include travel. It does not include tournaments. It does not include ID camps.

## What goes on top

For a kid playing in ECNL, MLS Next, or Girls Academy in San Diego, the annual out-of-pocket additions typically include:

- **Match kit and training kit**: $200–$400 a season. Often refreshes every one or two seasons.
- **Cleats, ball, shin guards, training bag**: $200–$400 a year baseline, more for growth-spurt replacement.
- **Tournament fees**: $200–$500 per weekend tournament, with multiple per season.
- **Showcase events**: $300–$800 per event, including travel.
- **ID camps**: $250–$600 per camp; serious college-aspirant players attend several per year.
- **Travel**: Out-of-region competitions are routine at MLS Next, ECNL, and GA levels. Hotel nights, fuel, food, time off work. **Rough range: $1,500 to $4,000 a year.**
- **Private training (optional but common)**: $80–$150 an hour with weekly cadence at top tiers.

Add it up and the **annual all-in cost for a competitive ECNL, MLS Next, or Girls Academy player in San Diego County typically lands between $8,000 and $20,000 per year**, depending on the kid's national-team aspirations, the travel intensity of their specific club, and how aggressively the family invests in ID camps and private training.

## What that adds up to over a career

A kid who plays at this level from U13 to U18 — six competitive years — typically absorbs **$48,000 to $120,000 of cumulative family investment**. That is not a typo. The lower end of that range is for a competitive but cost-disciplined household. The upper end is for a college-aspirant household running the full optionality at the higher-cost clubs.

This is the household-level expenditure that produces, in the United States, the players who appear on national-team rosters, ECNL/MLS Next/GA national finals, and college recruitment lists.

## Who gets to be at this level

In practice: kids whose families can absorb $8,000-to-$20,000-a-year participation costs.

The talent does not respect household income. The system does. By the time a U18 player makes a national-team selection pool or earns a Division I college soccer scholarship, the underlying talent has been filtered through six-plus years of cost-wall selection. Kids who couldn't afford to stay in the pipeline are simply no longer in the pool.

This is the structural feature of U.S. youth soccer that most resembles a household-income screen, and it is the feature Grassroots Foundation is specifically designed to address.

## Are scholarships available at the club level?

**Yes**, but at smaller scale than the demand. Most reputable San Diego competitive clubs operate some form of internal scholarship or fee-reduction program — sometimes labeled as financial aid, sometimes as tier discount, sometimes as a quiet conversation with the director of coaching. These programs exist and do real work. They also tend to be:

- **Smaller than the need.** A typical club may have funding for 1–3 full scholarships and a small number of partial reductions per age band.
- **Variable.** Renewable status is often discretionary, year to year.
- **Constrained to tuition.** Most internal scholarships cover the registration tuition line. Kit, travel, tournaments, ID camps, and other costs typically remain with the family.

Grassroots Foundation is designed to _complement_ these internal programs, not replace them. We pay tuition to partner clubs that already have full-scholarship slots filled, expand the dollars available, and cover the cost lines that internal scholarships typically don't reach.

ECNL, MLS Next, and Girls Academy are the highest-level competitive youth-soccer pathways in the United States. They are also the most expensive.
