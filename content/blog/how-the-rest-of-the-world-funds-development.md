The United States is, by participation, one of the largest youth-soccer markets in the world. By cost-per-player, it is also the most expensive. Those two facts are related, and the relationship is structural — not cultural, not a matter of "Americans paying for quality," not a temporary distortion.

It is the way the system was built.

This piece is a walk through how the rest of the world actually funds youth-soccer development, where the funding comes from, and what that means for who gets to play.

## Europe — federation and club integration

In England, Spain, Germany, Italy, and France, the dominant funding model rests on two pillars: a strong community-club layer with low fees, and a professional-club youth-academy layer that operates at zero cost to the family.

**Community clubs** — the place where the vast majority of kids actually play — charge annual dues in the range of **€50 to €300**. That fee covers a season's worth of training, matches, coaching, league registration, and basic kit support. The clubs are typically non-profits or community associations, run by a combination of volunteers and modestly-paid coaches, and they accept any kid in the geographic catchment.

**Professional-club academies** — the next layer up — operate as fully-funded development pipelines. A selected player at a Bundesliga, La Liga, Premier League, or Ligue 1 academy pays nothing. Training, matches, kit, equipment, education support, and in some cases housing are funded by the senior club, because those clubs are commercial entities that benefit financially from developing their own players.

The gap between the two tiers is real, but it's a _talent_ selection gap, not a household-budget gap. A kid who has the ability moves up. A kid who doesn't stays at community level, paying community-level dues.

## Mexico — community first, club youth after

Most Mexican kids play through **community ligas amateurs** — affordable neighborhood leagues with small fees, sometimes a few hundred pesos per season. These leagues run in nearly every Mexican city, with deep cultural and family integration. The bulk of kids play here, and they play well.

Talent identified in the community system gets pulled into **Liga MX club youth systems** — the Tigres, Monterrey, Pachuca, Chivas, América, and others — where signed youth players train and play **without family fees**. The clubs cover costs because, as in Europe, they're commercial operations that benefit from developing players.

This is the same two-tier structure as Europe: cheap base, free top. Different cultural texture, same financial geometry.

## CONCACAF — federation programs and small-club training

In Costa Rica, Honduras, Jamaica, Trinidad, El Salvador, and most of the rest of the region, most kids play through community leagues with **minimal fees**. Federation development programs and small-club training make up the bulk of organized soccer. Competitive selection is a separate, free track — kids who progress are identified and developed within federation or small-club programs that do not depend on family payments to operate.

The U.S.-scale pay-to-play model is not the regional norm anywhere in CONCACAF except inside the U.S. itself.

## South America — community, public fields, club academies

Across Brazil, Argentina, Colombia, Uruguay, and most of the continent, the dominant participation layer is **community leagues, public fields, and barrio teams.** Kids play for the equivalent of next to nothing. Talent identified inside that ecosystem gets pulled into **club youth systems** — Boca, River, Flamengo, Palmeiras, Peñarol, Nacional, and dozens of others — where, again, players pay nothing.

Pay-to-play at the U.S. scale doesn't exist here. It would not be culturally accepted as a structure, and it would not be economically possible at the household level for most families.

## Canada — closer to the U.S. than most assume

Canada is the regional outlier in the other direction: it follows the U.S. model more closely than any other country. Community soccer in Canada is reasonably accessible — typically a few hundred Canadian dollars a season — but competitive tiers run through provincial associations where families pay **$1,500 to $4,000+ a year**.

Cheaper than the U.S., but recognizably the same structure. Pay-to-play, lighter version. Notably, Canada's senior national team performance gap relative to its population base is one of the things people in Canadian soccer media talk about most often. The cost wall is the throughline.

## What this tells you about the U.S. model

The United States is not "expensive because soccer is expensive." Soccer is structurally cheap in most countries. The cost is a function of who pays — and in the U.S., the entire stack is pushed to the household. Tuition pays for coaches. Tournament fees pay for tournaments. Travel pays for travel. There is no community-funded layer at scale, no senior-club academy subsidy at scale, no federation development funding that comparable football nations rely on.

When you push the entire developmental pipeline onto the household balance sheet, you get the outcome the United States has. A talent pool filtered by household income. A drop-off in participation at the age the developmental window opens. A national-team selection pool that does not draw evenly from the geographic and demographic base producing the talent.

The structural fix is hard, because it would require either:

- A meaningful federation-funded community-soccer layer at low household cost, or
- A meaningful senior-club academy layer that funds youth development out of senior-club commercial revenue, or
- A non-profit philanthropic layer that closes the gap one kid at a time.

The first two require institutional reform that's currently not on the table. The third is the one anyone can start working on today.

## Where Grassroots Foundation fits

This is the third path. We are not pretending to be a federation. We are not pretending to be a Liga MX academy. We are the non-profit philanthropic layer that, in San Diego County, takes the household budget out of the equation for need-qualified kids.

The number we can fund is small relative to the size of the problem. The first cohort is 100 kids. The next cohort is bigger. The cohort after that is bigger again. None of these numbers are the whole answer. They are a directional fix that, if it works in San Diego, is replicable elsewhere — and worth more, structurally, than the dollar value of any single cohort.

## The honest read

The U.S. youth-soccer cost wall is not inevitable. It exists because of how the system was structured. The rest of the major footballing world chose differently, and their participation costs reflect that.

We can't change the structure of U.S. youth soccer with a small non-profit in San Diego County. We _can_ demonstrate that the cost wall is closable, one kid at a time, in a way that's transparent, accountable, and replicable. That's what Class of '26 is.

The first 100 are how it starts.
