If you ask a parent what club soccer costs, you'll usually get one of two answers. Either _"a couple thousand a season"_ — which is technically accurate for the bottom of the tuition column — or _"more than I can talk about,"_ which is the all-in number and is often closer to the truth.

Both answers are right. They just describe different parts of a stack that almost no one in the system writes down in one place.

This post is that one place.

## The number most clubs quote you

Club soccer tuition in San Diego County runs roughly **$2,000 to $5,000 per season** at competitive tiers. That covers coaching, field time, league registration, referee fees, and the cost of running the team. It is the number on the registration form and the number on the autopay receipt, and for many families it is the _only_ number they hear before signing up.

It's also the floor.

## What gets added on top

Tuition is the first cost in the season, not the last. By the time a kid completes a calendar year of competitive play, the actual outlay typically includes:

- **Match-day kit and training kit.** Most clubs require season-specific apparel — match jersey, training shirt, shorts, socks, and a team bag — that rotates every one or two years. This runs **$150 to $300 per kid per season**.
- **Equipment.** Cleats, shin guards, ball, goalkeeper gear if applicable, training bag. Cleats alone run $60 to $200 a pair, and a growing 11-year-old can outgrow them mid-season. Goalkeepers carry the highest equipment cost — full kit can hit $300 to $400.
- **Travel.** Away games inside San Diego County are mostly drivable, but competitive teams at NPL, DPL, MLS Next, ECNL, and Girls Academy levels routinely travel out of region. Hotel nights, fuel, food, and time off work add up fast.
- **Tournaments and showcase events.** A single weekend tournament can cost $200 to $500 in registration alone, before travel. Showcase events that recruiters attend run higher.
- **ID camps.** Optional, but presented as essential to families who think they need to get noticed for college. $250 to $600 per camp is normal; some kids attend several a year.
- **Private training.** Individual sessions with a coach, run between $80 and $150 an hour, with weekly cadence at the higher competitive levels.

Add it up at the top of the system and you reach **$8,000 to $20,000 a year per kid** at ECNL, MLS Next, and Girls Academy. The middle of the stack — competitive but non-elite tiers — lands somewhere between **$4,000 and $11,000**.

The $11,000 in the headline isn't a worst-case. It's a middle case.

## Why the all-in cost is hard to find

There's no industry-wide reporting requirement. Clubs disclose tuition, because they have to invoice it. Travel, kit, tournaments, ID camps, and private training are all out-of-pocket, distributed across the season, and rarely added up in one place.

A family discovers the all-in number the way they discover any cumulative cost: in the third month of the season, when the spring kit drops and the away-tournament hotel block opens and the coach mentions a regional ID camp in the same week.

This isn't anyone's bad behavior. It's just how the math is structured.

## Who this prices out

Every cost is denominated in dollars, but the _barrier_ is a household cash-flow problem. A family that can write a single $4,000 check at the start of a season is doing different work than a family who has the same total budget spread across the year. Tuition is often the easiest line because clubs offer payment plans. Kit, travel, tournaments, and showcase events arrive on a schedule the family didn't set.

Working-class families in San Diego — and particularly families in South Bay, where median household incomes sit well below the county-wide median — are pricing out long before they reach the bottom of the stack. The kid is good enough. The team wants them. The family budget runs out somewhere between the kit drop and the first tournament weekend.

## What the gap actually looks like

In San Diego club soccer, the most common drop-off point sits between **U12 and U14**. This is also the developmental window where the rest of competitive youth soccer is accelerating — longer field, full-size game, the start of the developmental academy track. It is, in other words, the window where staying in the system matters most for a kid's trajectory.

Children who would otherwise still be playing are leaving the game at the exact moment the system needs them most. The reason isn't talent. It isn't interest. It is, almost always, the budget.

## The honest read

Most U.S. youth sports systems run on a quiet assumption: that the family can absorb the stack. For a meaningful percentage of San Diego families, the assumption is wrong. The kid still wants to play. The team still has a spot. The talent is still there.

The bill is the gap. Closing it is the work.
