The pattern is consistent everywhere it's been measured. Kids start playing organized sports at age 6 or 7. Participation climbs through age 10. And then, between roughly **11 and 13**, the participation curve bends down hard.

This is not a story specific to soccer. It shows up in every U.S. youth sport that's tracked. The Aspen Institute's Project Play has been publishing the numbers for over a decade, and they've moved very little: the average American child quits organized team sports by age 11. By 13, more than half of kids who once played regularly have stopped.

The question worth asking is _why_ — and whether the reasons are about the kids or about the system.

## What kids say when researchers ask them

Project Play's surveys of children who left organized sports return the same handful of reasons, year after year:

1. **"It wasn't fun anymore."**
2. **"It got too competitive."**
3. **"Practices took too much time."**
4. **"I had a coach I didn't like."**
5. **"Travel was too much."**

These read like child-level explanations, which they are. But every one of them is downstream of an adult-level structural choice.

"Not fun anymore" tracks closely with the moment competitive selection begins. "Too competitive" is the same moment, viewed by the kid who didn't get picked. "Too much travel" describes a tier of competition that families opted into because that's where the development is. The kids are reporting symptoms; the system is generating them.

## What the parents don't say out loud

The same surveys, asked of parents, surface a different set of pressures: cost, scheduling, sibling logistics, travel, and the cumulative effort of keeping a kid in a competitive program. Cost ranks in the top three reasons in every recent year of Project Play data, but it doesn't usually appear at the top — partly because no one wants to tell a researcher their kid quit because of money.

Inside a household, it surfaces differently. The family doesn't _quit_. The family says they're "taking a season off." Or they "want to focus on schoolwork." Or "the schedule is just too much right now." A season off becomes a year off, and a year off at 13 is the end of competitive participation for most kids, because the development gap to the kids who didn't leave is no longer reasonable to close.

This is how a structural cost problem gets reported as a free-time decision.

## Why 11 to 13 specifically

The age range is not random. Three things happen at once:

- **The price of the game goes up.** This is the window when competitive club tiers start in earnest in the U.S. soccer system — NPL, MLS Next, ECNL, Girls Academy, equivalent — and tuition jumps from "rec-plus" levels ($500 to $1,000 a season) into the $2,000-to-$5,000 range. Travel, tournaments, ID camps, and showcase events arrive on the same schedule.
- **The commitment goes up.** Four or five days of training a week becomes normal. Weekends fill with games and travel. Schedules at this age require a parent driver, a sibling logistics plan, and a household decision about which kid's activity gets priority.
- **The selection gets sharper.** Coaches start cutting players, moving them down a tier, or telling them to play elsewhere. Kids who were team starters at U10 become bench players at U12 and quit at U13.

For a kid whose family can absorb the cost stack and run the logistics, this is the moment the game gets better — faster, higher-stakes, more developmental. For a kid whose family can't, it's the moment the game ends.

## The two-track outcome

The system, at this point, splits into two tracks.

**Track one:** Talent pools at the top, where development continues. By the time a kid reaches U15 inside this track, they've absorbed roughly $30,000 to $60,000 of cumulative family investment, plus a stable training and competition environment, plus the developmental compounding of practicing with stronger players.

**Track two:** Everyone else. The kids who left at U12 or U13 don't come back. They get older, the developmental gap widens, and the option to re-enter the competitive pipeline closes. They either find another sport, find another activity, or drop out of structured youth sports entirely.

The first track is small. The second track is large. And the wall between them, in San Diego County, is denominated almost entirely in dollars.

## What this means for the developmental pipeline

When we ask "where is U.S. soccer's talent pool getting narrower?" the honest answer is: it's getting narrower at age 12. By the time we look at U18 national-team selection or college recruitment, the pool we're choosing from has already been filtered for half a decade by one thing, and it isn't talent.

It's the family balance sheet.

For a country with the population base of the United States, that's a structural problem. For a county like San Diego — with strong soccer culture, multiple competitive clubs, and a large working-class population that's tied to the game — it's also an addressable one.

## What we work on

Grassroots Foundation focuses scholarship dollars where the drop-off math is most acute: the U12-to-U14 window in San Diego County. This is the highest-leverage period to keep a kid in the game, because:

- It's the window where most kids quit, so a marginal kid kept on the roster represents a meaningful counter to the pattern.
- It's the window where development compounds, so an extra year or two of competitive play returns more than the same year at U9 or U10.
- It's the window where the family-side cost shock is largest, so a partial scholarship moves the needle in a way that the same dollars wouldn't a year earlier or a year later.

We're not pretending that fixing cost fixes everything else — the developmental pipeline has other gaps. But cost is the gap that's easiest to measure, easiest to attribute, and easiest to close one kid at a time.

## The honest read

The 11-to-13 drop-off is not a kids problem. It's a system problem that shows up in kids. The cost wall hits at the same age the developmental window opens, and the family math runs out at the moment the game is asking the most of them.

Closing the gap, for our part, is a roster of 100 kids in San Diego County by the start of the 2026 season. Other organizations are doing other parts of this work. This is the part we do.
