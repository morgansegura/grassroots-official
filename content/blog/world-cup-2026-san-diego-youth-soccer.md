The 2026 FIFA World Cup was the largest in tournament history: 48 teams, 16 host cities across three countries. Los Angeles was a host city. San Diego sits two hours south. For the first time in a generation, the world's most-watched sporting event played out in the same media market as our youth-soccer system.

It ended on July 19. There was real opportunity in it, and there was also a meaningful amount of marketing language deployed around it. This piece tries to separate the two, now that we can look back at it instead of forward to it.

## What the tournament actually does for a region

A World Cup brings four concrete things to a host region:

1. **Visibility.** Soccer becomes, briefly, the dominant story in mainstream U.S. media. Programs that have been operating quietly in the background of American sports culture get sustained attention for roughly six weeks.
2. **Money.** Host cities receive a meaningful economic injection — hospitality, retail, transport — and corporate sponsors put commercial dollars behind soccer-adjacent activations in the host markets.
3. **New participants.** Youth-soccer participation typically spikes in the year after a host-city World Cup. Kids see the tournament, want to play, and parents respond. This effect has been documented in every recent host country.
4. **Institutional attention.** Federations, philanthropic foundations, and corporate giving programs orient toward soccer in the year before and the year after the tournament. Funding becomes available that wasn't available a year earlier.

How much of that reached San Diego County specifically is not measurable yet. The tournament is weeks behind us, and the registration cycle it would affect hasn't finished. We will know more by next spring than we do now, and we would rather say that than guess.

## What it didn't do, even at its best

A World Cup is a media event. It did not, by itself, rewire the structural cost problem at the heart of U.S. youth soccer. A kid in South Bay who wanted to play competitive club soccer in 2024 is looking at the same registration tuition this fall. Watching the tournament didn't fund a season. Hosting in LA didn't fund a season. The visibility created a _window_ for funding to happen; it did not automatically create the funding.

This matters because every host country since 1990 has seen the same pattern: a participation spike, followed by a quiet attrition curve as new participants encounter the same financial gates that filtered kids out before the tournament. The bump is real. The bump is also temporary, unless the structural work happens during the bump.

That is the part worth sitting with. The tournament was the easy half of the window, and it's the half that just closed.

## Why the window still matters

What a World Cup gives a regional youth-soccer ecosystem is roughly **18 to 24 months of unusual attention**: twelve months out from the tournament, six weeks of the tournament itself, and six to twelve months of aftermath. We are in the aftermath now. Inside it, three things are still easier than they normally are:

- **Fundraising.** Corporate, foundation, and major-donor giving in the host markets is oriented toward soccer. Pitches that would have struggled in 2023 land better in 2026.
- **Recruiting.** Volunteers, board members, advisors, and partner-club relationships are easier to develop. People still want to be part of the moment.
- **Storytelling.** Press interest in regional soccer programs is elevated. A program doing quiet work can tell its story to a wider audience than it usually gets.

For Grassroots Foundation, this window is the reason Class of '26 is named what it's named. The first cohort of 100 funded scholarships is targeted to be on partner-club rosters for the **2026–27 competitive season**, which begins in late summer, weeks after the final. The timing isn't decorative. It maps to the stretch where regional attention, fundraising, and recruitment are amplified at once.

## San Diego's specific position

San Diego County was not a host city, but its proximity to Los Angeles made it an effective extension of the LA host environment. Three specifics worth naming:

**The fan base is here.** San Diego has one of the densest soccer-fan populations in the United States, with deep cultural connection to Mexico, which hosted matches in Mexico City, Guadalajara, and Monterrey. The cross-border viewing experience was substantial.

**The youth-soccer infrastructure is here.** San Diego County has dozens of established competitive clubs operating at NPL, DPL, MLS Next, ECNL, Girls Academy, and equivalent tiers. The roster capacity to absorb a new cohort of scholarship-funded kids already exists. We are not building new programs, we are funding kids into existing ones.

**The need is here.** The cost wall facing working-class families in South Bay, East County, and other parts of the region did not get smaller because of the tournament. If anything it got more visible, because a summer like that one creates aspiration, and the aspiration collides with the same household-budget reality it always did.

## What we're doing with what's left of the window

Grassroots Foundation never treated the World Cup as the strategy. The strategy is the same one it would be without a host-city tournament: identify need-qualified families in San Diego County, partner with established clubs, and pay tuition, kit, equipment, and clinic costs directly. The tournament was _the timing_, not the _thesis_.

What the timing bought us, specifically:

- **A naming construct that resonates.** _Class of '26_ is, in donor and partner conversations, a clearer ask than _general scholarship fund_. It maps to a moment the whole region was already paying attention to.
- **A fundraising window that is still open.** The twelve months after the tournament are when major-donor and corporate-foundation attention stays available in the host region. That's the stretch we're in.
- **A starting cohort size that fits the moment.** One hundred kids is large enough to be visible, small enough to fund honestly, and specific enough to measure against.

## What we're _not_ doing with it

A few specifics, because the marketing temptation is real:

- We are not claiming the tournament solved, or will solve, U.S. pay-to-play.
- We did not run, and will not run, a "World Cup 2026" hospitality program for donors.
- We are not claiming that hosting in LA changed San Diego's structural cost picture.
- We are not a temporary organization that closes now that the tournament has. Class of '26 is the first cohort. There will be a Class of '27, a Class of '28, and so on. The window mattered; it was never the whole runway.

## The honest read

The World Cup was a real tailwind for U.S. youth soccer in the host regions. It was not, by itself, the fix. The fix is the boring, repeatable work of pulling the cost wall down one program, one club, one kid at a time. The tournament gave us an unusual amount of attention and a clearer name for the first cohort. The structural problem is what's standing there after the confetti.

The Cup left in July. The cost wall didn't. Tryouts and registration for the 2026–27 season are happening right now, which means the families who were priced out last year are being priced out again this month, in real time, while the coverage moves on.

The founding-donor block is the first 25 names on the Class of '26 roster, and those seats are open. That's the part of this moment anyone can actually move on. The rest of it was media.
