The 2026 FIFA World Cup is the largest in tournament history — 48 teams, 16 host cities across three countries, more than a billion viewers. Los Angeles is a host city. San Diego sits two hours south. For the first time in a generation, the world's most-watched sporting event will play out in the same media market as our youth-soccer system.

There is real opportunity in this. There is also a meaningful amount of marketing language being deployed around it. This piece tries to separate the two.

## What the tournament actually does for the region

A World Cup brings four concrete things to a host region:

1. **Visibility.** Soccer becomes, briefly, the dominant story in mainstream U.S. media. Programs that have been operating quietly in the background of American sports culture get sustained attention for roughly six weeks.
2. **Money.** Host cities receive a meaningful economic injection — hospitality, retail, transport — and corporate sponsors put commercial dollars behind soccer-adjacent activations in the host markets.
3. **New participants.** Youth-soccer participation typically spikes in the year after a host-city World Cup. Kids see the tournament, want to play, and parents respond. This effect has been documented in every recent host country.
4. **Institutional attention.** Federations, philanthropic foundations, and corporate giving programs orient toward soccer in the year before and the year after the tournament. Funding becomes available that wasn't available a year earlier.

For San Diego County's youth-soccer ecosystem, this is a tailwind. Not a magic wand, but a tailwind.

## What it doesn't do, even at its best

A World Cup is a media event. It doesn't, by itself, rewire the structural cost problem at the heart of U.S. youth soccer. A kid in South Bay who wanted to play competitive club soccer in 2024 will face the same registration tuition in 2027 unless someone explicitly addresses the cost stack. Watching the tournament won't fund a season. Hosting in LA won't fund a season. The visibility creates the _window_ for funding to happen; it doesn't automatically create the funding.

This matters because every host country since 1990 has seen the same pattern: a participation spike, followed by a quiet attrition curve as the new participants encounter the same financial gates that filtered out kids before the tournament. The bump is real. The bump is also temporary, unless the structural work happens during the bump.

## Why the window matters

What the World Cup gives a regional youth-soccer ecosystem is roughly **18 to 24 months of unusual attention** — twelve months out from the tournament, six months of the tournament itself, and six to twelve months of aftermath. Inside that window, three things become easier than they normally are:

- **Fundraising.** Corporate, foundation, and major-donor giving in the host markets orients toward soccer. Pitches that would have struggled in 2023 land in 2026.
- **Recruiting.** Volunteers, board members, advisors, and partner-club relationships are easier to develop. People want to be part of the moment.
- **Storytelling.** Press coverage of regional soccer programs increases. A program that's been doing quiet work for years can suddenly tell its story to a much wider audience.

For Grassroots Foundation, this window is the reason Class of '26 is named what it's named. The first cohort of 100 funded scholarships is intentionally targeted to be on partner-club rosters by the start of the **2026 competitive season** — which begins in late summer 2026, weeks after the World Cup final. The timing isn't decorative. It maps to the window where regional attention, fundraising, and recruitment are all amplified at once.

## San Diego's specific position

San Diego County is not a host city, but its proximity to Los Angeles makes it an effective extension of the LA host environment. Three specifics worth naming:

**The fan base is here.** San Diego has one of the densest soccer-fan populations in the United States, with deep cultural connection to Mexico, where the tournament will host matches in Mexico City, Guadalajara, and Monterrey. The cross-border viewing experience will be substantial.

**The youth-soccer infrastructure is here.** San Diego County has dozens of established competitive clubs operating at NPL, DPL, MLS Next, ECNL, Girls Academy, and equivalent tiers. The roster capacity to absorb a new cohort of scholarship-funded kids already exists — we are not building new programs, we are funding kids into existing ones.

**The need is here.** The cost wall affecting working-class families in South Bay, East County, and other parts of the region has not gotten smaller in the run-up to the tournament. If anything, it has gotten more visible — because the tournament creates more aspiration, which collides with the same household-budget reality.

## What we're doing with the window

Grassroots Foundation is not pretending that the World Cup is, by itself, the strategy. The strategy is the same strategy it would be without a host-city tournament: identify need-qualified families in San Diego County, partner with established clubs, and pay tuition, kit, equipment, and clinic costs directly. The tournament is _the timing_, not the _thesis_.

What the timing buys us, specifically:

- **A naming construct that resonates.** _Class of '26_ is, in donor and partner conversations, a clearer ask than _general scholarship fund_. It maps to a tournament moment that everyone in the region is already paying attention to.
- **A clearer fundraising window.** The 12 months leading up to summer 2026, and the 12 months after, are when major-donor and corporate-foundation attention will be most available in the host region.
- **A starting cohort size that fits the moment.** One hundred kids is large enough to be visible, small enough to be funded honestly, and specific enough to attribute outcomes against. It also fits the tournament construct in a way that, say, _fifty kids_ wouldn't.

## What we're _not_ doing with the window

A few specifics, because the marketing temptation is real:

- We are not claiming we will solve U.S. pay-to-play because of the tournament.
- We are not running a "World Cup 2026" hospitality program for donors.
- We are not promising that hosting in LA will, by itself, change San Diego's structural cost picture.
- We are not building a temporary organization that closes when the tournament ends. Class of '26 is the first cohort. There will be a Class of '27, a Class of '28, and so on. The window matters; it isn't the whole runway.

## The honest read

The World Cup is a real tailwind for U.S. youth soccer in the host regions. It is not, by itself, the fix. The fix is the boring, repeatable work of pulling the cost wall down one program, one club, one kid at a time. The tournament gives us an unusual amount of attention and a clearer naming construct for the first cohort. The structural problem starts after that.

If you're reading this in the spring or summer of 2026, the window is open right now. The first 25 names on the Class of '26 roster — the founding-donor block — are being funded between now and the start of the season.

That's the part of this moment we can move on. The rest of it is media.
